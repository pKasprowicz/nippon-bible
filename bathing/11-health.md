# 11. Bathing Safely

Every onsen must post the health information set by the Ministry of the Environment (Chapter 3). The current standard is a notice of **1 July 2014** (MoE 2014b). This chapter summarises it in plain English. It is general guidance, not medical advice: if you have a heart, lung or blood-pressure condition, ask your doctor before a course of hot baths.

## 11.1 When not to bathe

The notice defines **contraindications** (禁忌症, *kinkishō*) as illnesses or conditions in which **even a single bath or drink** of spring water may cause harm. For bathing in any onsen they are (MoE 2014b):

- the **active phase of an illness**, especially with **fever**;
- active **tuberculosis**, advanced **malignant tumours**, severe **anaemia** or other marked physical weakness;
- **serious heart or lung disease** in which slight movement causes breathlessness;
- **serious kidney disease** with swelling;
- **bleeding from the digestive tract**, or any visible bleeding;
- an **acute flare-up** of a chronic illness.

Some spring types add their own. **Acidic springs** and **sulphur springs** are contraindicated for people with sensitive skin or mucous membranes, and for elderly people with dry skin (MoE 2014b). Even where a contraindication applies, treatment under a doctor with specialist knowledge is not excluded (MoE 2014b).

## 11.2 Before the bath

From the notice (MoE 2014b):

- **Avoid bathing just before or after a meal, and after drinking alcohol.** Above all, do not bathe drunk.
- **Rest** when you are very tired, and for about **30 minutes after exercise**.
- **Elderly people, children and people with disabilities** should preferably not bathe alone.
- **Pour water over yourself** from the hands and feet first (*kakeyu*) to get used to the temperature, and wash.
- **Drink a glass of water** beforehand, especially just after waking up, to avoid dehydration.

## 11.3 How hot, how long, how often

From the notice (MoE 2014b):

| Question | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Temperature | Elderly people, people with high blood pressure or heart disease, and people who have had a stroke should avoid baths of **42 °C or more**. |
| Depth | People with weak heart or lung function should prefer a **half-body or partial bath** to full immersion. |
| How often | **1–2 baths a day** for the first few days; later up to **2–3**. |
| How long | At first **3–10 minutes** per bath, depending on the temperature; later up to **15–20 minutes**. |

## 11.4 In and after the bath

From the notice (MoE 2014b):

- **Keep still**, moving your hands and feet only lightly.
- **Get out slowly**, to avoid dizziness when you stand up.
- **If you feel dizzy or unwell,** call someone near you for help, get out slowly keeping your head low, and lie down until you recover.
- **Afterwards,** do not wash the spring off; dry yourself with a towel, dress, keep warm and **rest for about 30 minutes**. People with sensitive skin should rinse off strong springs such as acidic or sulphur springs, and chlorinated water.
- **Drink a glass of water.**
- **No towels in the bath**, to keep the water clean.

## 11.5 *Yuatari*: bath sickness

**湯あたり** (*yuatari*) is the Ministry's term for a reaction during a course of bathing. About **three days to a week** after starting, malaise, sleeplessness, digestive symptoms or skin inflammation may appear. While they last, **stop bathing or bathe less often** until you recover (MoE 2014b).

## 11.6 Drinking the water

Some onsen have a fountain for drinking. The notice sets rules for that too (MoE 2014b):

- **Take a doctor's advice** for a drinking cure; if you are on medication, ask your own doctor.
- **Under 15s** should in principle not drink spring water.
- **Drink only at the designated place**, fresh water drawn directly from the source.
- **One serving** is generally about **100–150 mL**; the total in a day about **200–500 mL**. Strongly acidic water (below pH 3) must be diluted first.
