# Hiragana Course: Start Here

Hiragana is the Japanese "alphabet" of 46 sounds. Learn it and you can read
station names on many signs, the readings printed above difficult kanji,
menu words like **すし** and **うどん**, the **ゆ** on hot-spring curtains, and
every grammar ending in a sentence.

This course gets you **reading** hiragana at a traveller's level in about
**8 sessions of 20–30 minutes**, plus **5 minutes of review a day**. You
won't need to write by hand.

## 1. What you'll be able to do

- Recognise all 46 basic characters, plus **゛/゜** variants and combinations like **きょ**
- Read station names such as **しぶや**, **うえの** and **とうきょう** from a sign
- Read food words, vending-machine labels (**つめたい** / **あたたかい**), and **いりぐち** / **でぐち**
- Sound out unfamiliar words, and spot the long vowels and double consonants that change meaning

## 2. How this course works (and why)

Each unit uses learning techniques that research consistently shows work.
None of them are tricks, and together they explain why the exercises look the way they do.

| Technique | What the research says | How this course uses it |
|-----------|-----------------------|-------------------------|
| **Testing yourself** (retrieval practice) | Recalling something from memory strengthens it far more than re-reading it. Students who tested themselves remembered much more a week later (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). | Almost everything is a question, not a reading list. |
| **Spacing** | Reviews spread over days beat the same time crammed into one session (Cepeda et al., 2006). | The **Daily review** schedules each character at growing intervals: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and 32 days. |
| **Pictures + sounds** (dual coding, mnemonics) | Linking a shape to a vivid image and a sound gives memory two routes to the answer (Paivio; picture mnemonics for kana have been studied with similar results). | Each character has a memory aid, and you can tap it to hear it. |
| **Guessing first** (pretesting) | Trying to answer *before* you're taught, even if you get it wrong, makes the lesson stick better (Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009). | Each unit opens with a short "guess first" quiz. Wrong guesses are expected. |
| **Mixing old and new** (interleaving) | Mixing items forces you to tell them apart, which builds stronger discrimination than practising one kind at a time (Rohrer & Taylor, 2007). | Practice mixes earlier units' characters in with the new ones. |
| **Look-alike contrast** | Comparing confusable items side by side teaches the differences that matter. | "Tell them apart" drills for pairs such as **き/さ**, **ね/れ/わ** and **る/ろ**. |
| **Desirable difficulty** | Practice that feels a little hard (typing the answer rather than recognising it) builds more durable memory (Bjork). | Each session goes from *read* → *find* → *listen* → *type*. |
| **Immediate, explained feedback** | Feedback that tells you *why* corrects errors and stops them sticking. | Wrong answers show the memory aid for both the right answer and the one you chose. |
| **Small chunks** (cognitive load) | Working memory holds only a few new items at once (Sweller). | 5–11 new characters per unit, and real words use only characters you've already met. |
| **Fluency** | Reading signs from a moving train needs speed, not just accuracy. | Timed speed rounds once you're accurate. |
| **Adaptive practice** | Practice time pays off most when it goes to what you don't know yet. | An **endless game** after every unit serves your weakest characters most, and shows each character's proficiency live. |
| **Sleep** | Memory consolidates during sleep. | One unit per day, and the review the next morning. |

> **The golden rule:** a mistake during practice isn't a failure. It shows you exactly what
> needs a second look, and it'll come back a few questions later.

## 4. How to use the exercises

- **Say every answer out loud** before you click. Speaking is a form of recall, and it trains your ear.
- **Tap any Japanese text** in the lessons to hear it. Exercises play sounds automatically.
  If you hear nothing, your device may need a Japanese voice (in iOS, Android, Windows or macOS
  speech settings). Listening exercises switch to reading exercises when no voice is available.
- **Keyboard:** <kbd>1</kbd>–<kbd>4</kbd> to answer, <kbd>Enter</kbd> to continue, <kbd>R</kbd> to replay a sound.
- **Typing answers:** any common spelling works: *shi* or *si*, *tsu* or *tu*, *fu* or *hu*.
- Your progress is saved **in this browser only**. Nothing is sent anywhere.

## 5. Try it now: how much can you guess?

This quiz is meant to be guessed. It warms up your brain for what's coming.

```quiz
title: Guess first
? Hiragana and the Latin alphabet differ in one basic way. Each hiragana character usually stands for…
- a single letter, like "k"
- a whole syllable, like "ka" *
- a whole word
> Most hiragana stand for a consonant plus a vowel (ka, shi, tsu), or just a vowel. That's why there are 46 of them and not 26.

? Which of these do you think is hiragana?
- 駅
- エキ
- えき *
> えき is hiragana: round and curvy. エキ is katakana (angular), and 駅 is the kanji. All three read "eki", station.

? On a hot-spring curtain you see one big character, ゆ. What might it mean?
- hot water *
- men only
- closed
> ゆ (yu) means hot water. You'll meet it in Unit 5.
```
