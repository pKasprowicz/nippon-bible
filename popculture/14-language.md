# 14. Language for Fans

Many people start learning Japanese because of anime, manga, games or music. Researchers note that "a large number of current learners are drawn to the study of Japanese through their interest in popular Japanese culture of animation (anime), manga, TV dramas, and films" (Jackson and Kennett 2021). This chapter helps such learners separate the Japanese of fiction from the Japanese of daily life, and collects the key words of this handbook.

## 14.1 Fiction's Japanese

Chapter 4 introduced ***yakuwarigo***, role language: ways of speaking that call up a stock character, such as the old professor's *washi … ja* or the young lady's *desu wa yo*. Kinsui Satoshi calls them a **"virtual reality" Japanese**. Role language is a linguistic stereotype that exists as shared knowledge, **not as language people naturally use**. Media spread it and fix it in place (Kinsui 2003 and 2000, cited in Figueira 2019).

Two consequences for learners:

1. **Recognise it.** Role language is efficient: it tells you instantly who a character is. Knowing it makes manga and anime easier to follow.
2. **Don't copy it.** Copying a stock character's speech makes you sound like that character. **Standard Japanese** is itself treated as the neutral reference point against which all other role languages stand out (Kinsui 2003, cited in Figueira 2019). It is the safe default for a learner.

## 14.2 "Men's" and "women's" sentence endings

Japanese textbooks often describe some sentence-final particles as masculine or feminine. Yan Yutong and Suzuki Kazunori summarise one standard description (Masuoka and Takubo 1992, cited in Yan and Suzuki 2024):

| Considered masculine | Considered feminine |
|---|---|
| よ, か, ぞ, のか | の, わ, わよ |

**Real usage is changing.** They counted sentence-final particles in ten novels that won the [Akutagawa Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akutagawa_Prize) between 2010 and 2022, five by men and five by women. They found "a nuanced shift". Gender-specific patterns "have undergone notable changes in the past decade", challenging earlier findings (Yan and Suzuki 2024).

**Fiction exaggerates.** Kinsui adds that in fiction politeness and refinement are themselves heard as feminine, and power as masculine. High-ranking women in dubbed and period dramas take on some "men's" forms (だ, imperatives, ぞ, な) but not others (ぼく, おれ, きさま, ぜ) (Kinsui 2023; Chapter 4).

What this means for you. The table lists what the research cited in this handbook says each feature signals:

| What you hear | What it signals | Source |
|---|---|---|
| わし, じゃ, おる | The old man; also gods and ghosts, by "projection" | Kinsui 2003, 2011 (Chapter 4) |
| 拙者, さよう | The samurai | Kinsui 2011 (Chapter 4) |
| わたくし … ですわよ | The well-bred young lady (*ojōsama*) | Kinsui 2011 (Chapter 4) |
| ぼく … のさ | The boy or young man | Kinsui 2011 (Chapter 4) |
| だ, imperatives, ぞ, prohibitive な | Status and power; used even by high-ranking women in fiction | Kinsui 2023 |
| ぼく, おれ, きさま, ぜ | Masculinity; not used by those women | Kinsui 2023 |
| ～わ, ～わよ | Described as feminine, but usage is changing | Yan and Suzuki 2024 |
| ～あるよ | The stock "Chinese" character: a pidgin stereotype from the treaty ports | Kinsui 2014 (Chapter 4) |

Because these are stereotypes of fiction, the safe choice for a learner remains standard Japanese, with the polite *desu/masu* forms taught in the main course.

## 14.3 Rude words and where not to learn them

Fans often want to understand insults and slang. Lachlan Jackson and Belinda Kennett analysed six commercially published guides to "bad" Japanese for learners (Jackson and Kennett 2021):

- **Worth learning.** Slang, swearing and taboo language are a low priority in most courses, but learners meet them and are curious. Non-polite language "may be more relevant to learners' communicative needs than polite registers".
- **Out of date.** Some guides are antiquated. One draws much of its material from the author's experience of the Occupation years.
- **Badly translated.** The English is often "far more extreme than the Japanese":
  - **頭にくるわ** (*atama ni kuru wa*) is not offensive and usually means "it makes me angry". One guide renders it with a crude English idiom.
  - **アホか？！** (*aho ka?!*) can be playful ("You idiot!", "Don't be silly") as well as insulting, so a single obscene English equivalent misrepresents it.
- **Their conclusion.** The guides have "serious shortcomings", and learners need more reliable references and a more formal approach to non-polite language.

Words heard in anime fights are best learned **for understanding**, not for use, until you can judge their weight in context.
