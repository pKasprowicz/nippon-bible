# Lesson 9: Food & Dishes

What the dishes are called, what goes into them and how they are made, so you
can read a menu, order with confidence and know what will arrive. Names are
given as **Japanese script · romaji · meaning**. Tap the Japanese on the website
to hear it.

Japanese cooking rests on a few ideas that explain a lot of what you'll eat:

- **Umami first.** Most savoury food is built on **出汁 · dashi · stock**, usually
  made from kelp and dried fish, and seasoned with fermented soy products.
  Flavours are clean rather than heavily spiced.
- **Rice is the meal.** ご飯 · *gohan* means both "cooked rice" and "meal".
  The classic home meal is **一汁三菜 · ichijū sansai**: rice, *one soup* and
  *three side dishes*, with pickles.
- **Season matters.** **旬 · shun** is the peak season of an ingredient. Menus
  change with it, and **季節限定 · kisetsu gentei** ("limited to the season")
  is everywhere, from fine restaurants to konbini.
- **Specialist restaurants.** Many places cook one thing only: a sushi bar, a
  ramen shop, a tonkatsu house, an eel restaurant. The sign over the door tells
  you what they serve, so this lesson is organised the same way.

## 1. The pantry: what Japanese food tastes of

### Dashi, the stock behind everything

| Dashi | Made from | Character |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| **合わせ出汁 · awase dashi** | 昆布 *konbu* (kelp) + 鰹節 *katsuobushi* (smoked, dried, fermented skipjack tuna, shaved into flakes) | The all-purpose stock: miso soup, noodle broths, simmered dishes |
| **昆布出汁 · konbu dashi** | Kelp only | Mild and sweet; the base of *yudōfu* and *shabu-shabu*, and vegetarian if nothing else is added |
| **煮干し出汁 · niboshi dashi** | Small dried sardines | Stronger and fishier; home miso soup, some ramen |
| **椎茸出汁 · shiitake dashi** | Dried shiitake mushrooms soaked in cold water | Deep and earthy; with kelp, the classic vegan stock |

**How first dashi (一番出汁 · ichiban dashi) is made:** a piece of kelp soaks in
cold water, then is heated slowly and lifted out **just before the water boils**:
boiled kelp turns slimy and bitter. A handful of bonito flakes goes in, the pot
comes off the heat, and after a minute or two, once the flakes sink, the stock is
strained **without squeezing** the flakes, which would cloud it. The used kelp and
flakes are simmered again for a stronger, cheaper **二番出汁 · niban dashi**, used
for miso soup and simmered dishes. The glutamate in the kelp and the inosinate in
the bonito strengthen each other, which is why the combination tastes far more
savoury than either alone.

### Seasonings

| Ingredient | What it is | Nuances |
|------------|------------|---------|
| **醤油 · shōyu · soy sauce** | Soybeans and wheat fermented with kōji mould, salt and water | **濃口 · koikuchi** is the standard dark sauce. **薄口 · usukuchi** (Kansai) is *lighter in colour but saltier*, used to keep food pale. **溜まり · tamari** uses little or no wheat (check the label if you avoid gluten). **白 · shiro** is very pale, mostly wheat. |
| **味噌 · miso** | Soybeans fermented with kōji and salt | **白味噌 · shiro miso** is pale, sweet and briefly aged (Kyoto's *saikyō miso*). **赤味噌 · aka miso** is darker, saltier and aged longer. **合わせ味噌 · awase miso** is a blend. **豆味噌 · mame miso** is soybeans only (e.g. *hatchō miso* from Okazaki, Aichi). **麦味噌 · mugi miso** uses barley kōji (Kyūshū). |
| **味醂 · mirin** | Sweet rice seasoning | *Hon-mirin* (本みりん) is about 14% alcohol and adds gloss and gentle sweetness. Cheaper *mirin-fū* (みりん風) has almost no alcohol. |
| **料理酒 · ryōrishu** | Cooking sake | Removes fishy smells, adds depth. |
| **酢 · su** | Rice vinegar | Mild; seasons sushi rice and *sunomono* salads. |
| **麹 · kōji** | Rice (or barley, or soybeans) grown with the mould *Aspergillus oryzae* | The engine behind miso, soy sauce, sake, mirin and vinegar. Also sold as **塩麹 · shio-kōji**, a salty marinade. |
| **山葵 · wasabi** | Grated rhizome of the wasabi plant | Real wasabi is grated fresh, traditionally on sharkskin, and fades within minutes. Most tube and sachet "wasabi" is horseradish and mustard, coloured green. |
| **七味唐辛子 · shichimi tōgarashi** | Seven-spice mix around chilli: typically sanshō, sesame, citrus peel, hemp seed, seaweed, ginger | Shaken on noodles and *gyūdon*. **一味 · ichimi** is chilli only. |
| **山椒 · sanshō** | Ground seed pods of the Japanese prickly ash | Citrusy and slightly numbing; the classic on grilled eel. |
| **柚子 · yuzu** | Fragrant citrus | Zest in soups, juice in **ポン酢 · ponzu** (citrus juice with soy sauce), **柚子胡椒 · yuzu koshō** (salty yuzu-chilli paste). |
| **海苔 · nori** | Sheets of dried, pressed seaweed | Wraps sushi rolls and rice balls. Crisp when fresh: konbini rice balls keep it separate until you open them. |
| **鰹節 · katsuobushi** | Shaved dried bonito | Also a topping: the flakes "dance" in the heat on top of *okonomiyaki* and *takoyaki*. |

### Pickles and other small things

- **漬物 · tsukemono · pickles** come with almost every set meal: **沢庵 · takuan**
  (yellow pickled daikon radish), **柴漬け · shibazuke** (purple, aubergine and
  cucumber with red shiso, from Kyoto), **糠漬け · nukazuke** (vegetables buried
  in fermenting rice bran), **浅漬け · asazuke** (lightly salted, quick pickles).
- **梅干し · umeboshi**: Japanese apricot (*ume*) salted and dried, often coloured
  red with shiso. Intensely sour and salty: a classic rice ball filling.
- **ガリ · gari**: thin slices of sweet-pickled young ginger, eaten between
  pieces of sushi to clean the palate. **紅生姜 · beni shōga** is the red, shredded
  ginger pickle on *gyūdon*, *yakisoba* and *takoyaki*.

## 2. Rice

Japanese rice is short-grain *japonica*: slightly sticky, so it can be eaten with
chopsticks and shaped into balls. At home it's measured in **合 · gō** (180 ml,
the rice-cooker cup).

**How it's cooked:** the rice is washed in several changes of water, stirring
gently, until the water runs nearly clear (this removes surface starch so the
grains don't turn gluey). It soaks for about 30 minutes, then cooks with a little
more water than rice by volume. After cooking it rests, lid on, for about ten
minutes (**蒸らし · murashi**, "steaming through") before it's fluffed with a
paddle. **Sushi rice** (**酢飯 · sumeshi** or **シャリ · shari**) is cooked
slightly firmer, then folded while hot with vinegar, sugar and salt in a wide
wooden tub and fanned so it turns glossy rather than wet.

### Sushi 寿司 · sushi

*Sushi* means the **vinegared rice**, not raw fish. Raw fish on its own, without
rice, is **刺身 · sashimi**. At a sushi bar the rice is *shari* and the topping
is **ネタ · neta**.

| Style | What it is |
|-------|------------|
| **握り · nigiri** | A hand-pressed finger of rice with a slice of topping; often a dab of wasabi in between. |
| **巻き · maki** | Rolled in nori with a bamboo mat. **細巻き · hosomaki** (thin, one filling, e.g. **鉄火巻 · tekkamaki** tuna, **かっぱ巻 · kappamaki** cucumber), **太巻き · futomaki** (thick, several fillings). Inside-out rolls (rice outside) are mostly a Western style. |
| **軍艦巻 · gunkan maki** | "Battleship": a nori wall around rice, holding loose toppings such as salmon roe or sea urchin. |
| **手巻き · temaki** | A hand-rolled cone of nori; eat it at once, while the nori is crisp. |
| **ちらし寿司 · chirashizushi** | Toppings scattered over a bowl of sushi rice. |
| **稲荷寿司 · inarizushi** | Sushi rice stuffed into a pouch of sweet simmered fried tofu. |
| **押し寿司 · oshizushi** | Pressed in a wooden box and cut into blocks; an Osaka tradition (e.g. **バッテラ · battera**, vinegared mackerel). |

**Edomae** (江戸前, "in front of Edo", old Tokyo) sushi grew up before
refrigeration, so much of the fish is **prepared** rather than simply sliced:
marinated in soy (**漬け · zuke**), cured between sheets of kelp
(**昆布締め · kobujime**), salted and vinegared (**締め · shime**, as in
**〆鯖 · shimesaba**), or simmered (eel, octopus). At a good counter the chef
brushes each piece with **煮切り · nikiri** (soy sauce reduced with sake and
mirin) or a pinch of salt: then **don't add soy sauce**. **お任せ · omakase** means
"I leave it to you": the chef serves a sequence of their choosing, usually from
lighter white fish to richer fish, with egg or a hand roll near the end.

**Common toppings (ネタ)**

| Topping | | Notes |
|---------|---|-------|
| 鮪 · maguro | tuna | **赤身 · akami** lean red meat · **中トロ · chūtoro** medium-fatty · **大トロ · ōtoro** the fattiest belly. **ネギトロ · negitoro** is minced fatty tuna with spring onion. |
| サーモン · sāmon | salmon | Raw salmon sushi is recent: it took off from the late 1980s with farmed Norwegian salmon. Now the most popular topping at conveyor-belt shops. |
| 鰤 · buri / はまち · hamachi | yellowtail | A "promotion fish" whose name changes with size: *hamachi* is the younger (and often the farmed) fish, *buri* the adult, at its best in winter (寒鰤 · *kanburi*). |
| 鯛 · tai | sea bream | Celebratory white fish. |
| 平目 · hirame | flounder | Delicate white fish; **縁側 · engawa** is the chewy, rich fin muscle. |
| 鯵 · aji | horse mackerel | Silver-skinned; often served with grated ginger and spring onion. |
| 鯖 · saba | mackerel | Usually salted and vinegared (*shimesaba*). |
| 小鰭 · kohada | gizzard shad | Salted and vinegared; the test of an Edomae chef's skill. |
| 烏賊 · ika · 蛸 · tako | squid · octopus | Octopus is boiled first. |
| 海老 · ebi | prawn | **甘海老 · amaebi** is raw and sweet; **車海老 · kurumaebi** is usually boiled. |
| 帆立 · hotate | scallop | Raw and sweet. |
| 雲丹 · uni | sea urchin | The soft orange gonads; sweet and briny. |
| イクラ · ikura | salmon roe | Marinated in soy; the word comes from Russian *ikra*. |
| 穴子 · anago | conger eel | Saltwater eel, simmered and brushed with a thick sweet sauce (**ツメ · tsume**). Different from *unagi* (see section 4). |
| 玉子 · tamago | rolled omelette | Sweet; traditionally the shop's signature. |

**Conveyor-belt sushi (回転寿司 · kaitenzushi):** plates are colour-coded by
price (the chart is on the wall), or you order on a touch screen and the dish
arrives on an express lane. Green tea powder and a hot-water tap are at your
seat: put a spoon of powder in the cup and add water. Stack your plates; staff
count them, or you drop them into a slot that counts them.

### Rice bowls 丼 · donburi

A bowl of rice with a topping, often simmered in a sweet-savoury mixture of
dashi, soy sauce and mirin. Names end in **-don**.

| Dish | How it's made |
|------|---------------|
| **牛丼 · gyūdon** | Thinly sliced beef and onion simmered in sweet soy-dashi broth. Fast-food staple (Yoshinoya, Sukiya, Matsuya). Add *beni shōga* and *shichimi*; order **つゆだく · tsuyudaku** for extra sauce. |
| **親子丼 · oyakodon** | "Parent and child": chicken and onion simmered in the broth, then beaten egg is poured over and cooked only until **half-set**, so it stays soft and creamy. |
| **かつ丼 · katsudon** | A pork cutlet (tonkatsu) simmered briefly in the same broth with onion and finished with egg in the same way. |
| **天丼 · tendon** | Tempura on rice, with a thick, sweet tempura sauce poured over. |
| **海鮮丼 · kaisendon** | Raw seafood over rice (plain or vinegared): a harbour-town and fish-market favourite. |
| **うな重 · unajū / 鰻丼 · unadon** | Grilled eel on rice; *-jū* comes in a lacquered box and is the grander version. |
| **豚丼 · butadon** | Grilled pork in a sweet soy glaze; the Obihiro (Hokkaidō) speciality. |

### Other rice dishes

- **おにぎり · onigiri · rice ball**: salted rice pressed into a triangle or ball
  around a filling and wrapped in nori. Classic fillings: **鮭 · sake/shake**
  (salted salmon), **梅 · ume** (umeboshi), **ツナマヨ · tsuna mayo** (tuna with
  mayonnaise), **昆布 · konbu** (kelp simmered in soy), **明太子 · mentaiko**
  (spicy pollock roe), **おかか · okaka** (bonito flakes with soy). In konbini,
  open the wrapper in the numbered order **1 → 2 → 3** so the nori slides out of
  its film without tearing.
- **カレーライス · karē raisu · curry rice**: arrived via the British navy in the
  late 1800s and became a national comfort food: a thick, mild, slightly sweet
  sauce from a roux of flour, fat and curry powder (sold as blocks for home
  cooking), with onion, carrot, potato and meat. **カツカレー · katsu karē** adds a
  pork cutlet. Chains such as CoCo Ichibanya let you choose the spiciness level.
- **オムライス · omuraisu**: rice fried with chicken and ketchup, wrapped in a thin
  omelette (or topped with a soft one that is split open), with more ketchup or
  demi-glace sauce. A **洋食 · yōshoku** dish (see section 7).
- **炒飯 · chāhan**: fried rice, Chinese style.
- **お茶漬け · ochazuke**: green tea or dashi poured over rice with a topping
  (salmon, umeboshi, seaweed); a light end to a night out.
- **炊き込みご飯 · takikomi gohan**: rice cooked together with dashi, soy sauce and
  seasonal ingredients (mushrooms, chestnuts, bamboo shoots). Cooked in an
  individual iron pot, it's **釜飯 · kamameshi**.
- **餅 · mochi**: steamed glutinous rice, pounded into a smooth, elastic dough.
  Eaten grilled, in soup (**お雑煮 · ozōni** at New Year) or as sweets. Cut it
  small and chew well: mochi causes choking accidents every New Year, mostly
  among the elderly.

## 3. Noodles 麺 · men

Slurping is normal: it cools the noodles and carries the aroma. Noodles come
**hot** in broth or **cold** with a dipping sauce; the menu will tell you which.

### Ramen ラーメン · rāmen

Ramen came from Chinese cooks in the early 20th century (it was first called
**支那そば · shina soba**, later **中華そば · chūka soba**) and is now Japan's
most argued-about food. A bowl has five parts, each made separately:

1. **スープ · sūpu · broth**: simmered from pork bones, chicken, dried fish,
   kelp or a mix. **清湯 · chintan** is clear, from gentle simmering.
   **白湯 · paitan** is cloudy and creamy: the bones are boiled hard for hours,
   so fat and collagen emulsify into the broth.
2. **タレ · tare · seasoning concentrate**: a few spoonfuls in the empty bowl
   that give the soup its salt and its name: **醤油 · shōyu** (soy sauce),
   **塩 · shio** (salt, the lightest), **味噌 · miso** (rich, often stir-fried
   with vegetables first).
3. **麺 · men · noodles**: wheat noodles made with **かん水 · kansui**, alkaline
   mineral water, which makes them yellowish, springy and resistant to going
   soft in hot soup. Thin and straight, or thick and wavy, chosen to suit the
   broth.
4. **香味油 · kōmiyu · aroma oil**: a spoon of flavoured fat (chicken fat, garlic
   oil, burnt-garlic *mayu*) floating on top.
5. **具 · gu · toppings**: **チャーシュー · chāshū** (braised or roasted pork,
   often rolled belly), **味玉 · ajitama** (soft-boiled egg marinated in soy, with
   a jammy yolk), **メンマ · menma** (fermented bamboo shoots), **ネギ · negi**
   (spring onion), **海苔 · nori**, **ナルト · naruto** (white fish cake with a pink
   spiral), **もやし · moyashi** (bean sprouts), **コーン · kōn** and **バター · batā**
   (corn and butter, on Sapporo miso ramen).

**Styles you'll meet**

| Style | Where | What makes it |
|-------|-------|---------------|
| **豚骨 · tonkotsu** | Hakata (Fukuoka), Kyūshū | Milky pork-bone paitan with thin, straight noodles. You choose the noodle firmness: **バリカタ · barikata** (very firm), **カタ · kata** (firm), **普通 · futsū** (regular), **やわ · yawa** (soft). Thin noodles go soft quickly, so portions are small and you order a refill of noodles, **替え玉 · kaedama**, into the remaining soup. |
| **味噌 · miso** | Sapporo, Hokkaidō | Miso stir-fried with vegetables and minced pork, thick wavy noodles, often corn and butter. Built for cold winters. |
| **醤油 · shōyu** | Tokyo and much of the country | Clear chicken or chicken-and-fish broth with soy tare; curly medium noodles. |
| **塩 · shio** | Hakodate and elsewhere | Clear and light; lets the broth speak. |
| **喜多方 · Kitakata** | Fukushima | Shōyu broth with thick, flat, wavy noodles; eaten even for breakfast. |
| **家系 · iekei** | Yokohama | Tonkotsu-shōyu with thick noodles, spinach and nori; you choose noodle firmness, fat level and broth strength. |
| **つけ麺 · tsukemen** | Everywhere | Thick noodles served **cold** (rinsed to firm them up), dipped into a concentrated hot soup. At the end, ask for **スープ割り · sūpu-wari**: staff dilute the leftover dip with hot broth so you can drink it. |
| **油そば / まぜそば · abura soba / mazesoba** | Tokyo | No soup: noodles tossed with tare and oil; add vinegar and chilli oil at the table. |

Many ramen shops use a **ticket machine (食券)**: buy your ticket, hand it over,
and say your noodle firmness if asked.

### Soba 蕎麦 · soba

Thin noodles of **buckwheat** flour, grey-brown and nutty. Buckwheat has no
gluten, so pure buckwheat dough is crumbly: most soba is **二八 · ni-hachi**,
80% buckwheat and 20% wheat flour as a binder. **十割 · jūwari** is 100%
buckwheat, harder to make and more fragrant. The dough is kneaded, rolled thin,
folded in layers and cut by hand with a large square-bladed knife into even
strands.

- **ざるそば / もりそば · zaru soba / mori soba**: chilled soba on a bamboo tray,
  dipped into cold **つゆ · tsuyu** (dashi, soy sauce, mirin), with spring onion
  and wasabi to stir in. *Zaru* is usually topped with shredded nori. At the end
  you'll get a pot of **蕎麦湯 · sobayu**, the starchy cooking water: pour it into
  your leftover dipping sauce and drink it as a soup.
- **かけそば · kake soba**: hot soba in hot broth. Toppings give the names:
  **天ぷらそば · tenpura soba** (with a tempura prawn), **月見 · tsukimi**
  ("moon viewing": a raw egg), **山菜 · sansai** (mountain vegetables),
  **鴨南蛮 · kamo nanban** (duck and leek).
- **年越しそば · toshikoshi soba**: eaten on New Year's Eve for a long life and
  to "cut off" the old year.
- **わんこそば · wanko soba** (Iwate): servers keep tipping single mouthfuls into
  your bowl until you put the lid on.

> **Buckwheat allergy** can be severe. Soba and udon are often boiled in the
> same water and served from the same kitchen. If you're allergic, say
> *Soba arerugī ga arimasu* and avoid soba shops altogether.

### Udon うどん · udon

Thick, white **wheat** noodles, soft or chewy (the springy bite is called
**コシ · koshi**). Kansai broths are pale and kelp-forward, seasoned with light
usukuchi soy; Kantō broths are dark, with more bonito and dark koikuchi soy.

| Dish | What it is |
|------|------------|
| **かけうどん · kake udon** | Hot broth, spring onion. The base for the rest. |
| **きつねうどん · kitsune udon** | With a sheet of sweet simmered fried tofu (*aburaage*); "fox" because foxes are said to love fried tofu. |
| **たぬき · tanuki** | Regional: in **Tokyo**, topped with **天かす · tenkasu** (crunchy tempura batter bits); in **Osaka**, *tanuki* means *soba* with fried tofu (the soba version of *kitsune*); in **Kyoto**, thickened broth with sliced fried tofu. |
| **釜玉 · kamatama** | Hot noodles straight from the pot, tossed with raw egg and a little soy sauce: a Sanuki speciality, like a Japanese carbonara. |
| **ぶっかけ · bukkake** | A small amount of strong broth poured over the noodles, hot or cold. |
| **カレーうどん · karē udon** | Udon in curry thickened with dashi. Splashes: wear dark clothes. |
| **鍋焼きうどん · nabeyaki udon** | Cooked and served in its own small pot with egg, chicken, tempura and vegetables. |

**Regional udon:** **讃岐 · Sanuki** (Kagawa, the udon prefecture: firm, chewy,
cheap, often self-service), **稲庭 · Inaniwa** (Akita: thin, hand-stretched and
dried, smooth), **きしめん · kishimen** (Nagoya: wide and flat), **伊勢うどん · Ise
udon** (very soft, with a thick dark sauce), **ほうとう · hōtō** (Yamanashi: flat
noodles stewed in miso with pumpkin and vegetables).

### Other noodles

- **素麺 · sōmen**: very thin wheat noodles, served ice-cold in summer with a
  dipping sauce. **流しそうめん · nagashi sōmen**: noodles flow down a bamboo
  flume of running water and you catch them with chopsticks.
- **焼きそば · yakisoba**: ramen-type noodles stir-fried with pork, cabbage and a
  sweet-tangy Worcestershire-style sauce, finished with *aonori* (green seaweed
  powder) and pickled ginger. A festival-stall classic.
- **冷やし中華 · hiyashi chūka**: summer cold ramen noodles with strips of ham,
  egg and cucumber, in a soy-vinegar or sesame dressing.
- **春雨 · harusame** (glass noodles of starch) and **しらたき · shirataki**
  (translucent konjac noodles, in sukiyaki).

## 4. Cooking methods, and the dishes made with them

The method is usually part of the dish's name, which makes menus easier to read.

| Kanji | Reading | Method |
|-------|---------|--------|
| 焼き | yaki | grilled, pan-fried, baked |
| 揚げ | age | deep-fried |
| 煮 / 煮込み | ni / nikomi | simmered / stewed |
| 蒸し | mushi | steamed |
| 炒め | itame | stir-fried |
| 生 | nama | raw |
| 和え | ae | tossed in a dressing |
| 酢の物 | sunomono | vinegared salad |
| 刺身 | sashimi | sliced raw |
| 炙り | aburi | seared with a flame |

### Deep-fried 揚げ物 · agemono

- **天ぷら · tenpura**: introduced through Portuguese contact in the 16th century
  and refined in Edo. Seafood and vegetables are dipped in a batter of **ice-cold
  water**, egg and low-gluten flour, **mixed only briefly** so that it stays
  lumpy: cold and little mixing keep gluten from developing, so the coating fries
  thin, lacy and crisp instead of bready. Fried at about 170–180 °C, a few pieces
  at a time. Eat at once, dipped in **天つゆ · tentsuyu** (dashi, soy, mirin) with
  grated daikon, or with salt (sometimes matcha salt). **かき揚げ · kakiage** is a
  fritter of shredded vegetables and small shrimp. At a tempura counter, each
  piece comes straight from the oil to your plate.
- **とんかつ · tonkatsu**: a thick pork cutlet coated in flour, beaten egg and
  coarse **パン粉 · panko** breadcrumbs, deep-fried until golden. **ロース · rōsu**
  (loin, with a rim of fat) or **ヒレ · hire** (fillet, lean and tender). Served
  sliced, with a mound of shredded raw cabbage (often refilled for free), a thick
  fruity **とんかつソース · tonkatsu sōsu**, hot mustard, rice and miso soup (often
  **豚汁 · tonjiru**, a hearty pork miso soup). Some shops give you sesame seeds
  to grind into the sauce in a small grooved bowl. Variations: **チキンカツ ·
  chikin katsu**, **メンチカツ · menchi katsu** (minced meat), **味噌カツ · miso
  katsu** (Nagoya, with a sweet red-miso sauce).
- **唐揚げ · karaage**: bite-sized chicken (usually thigh) marinated in soy sauce,
  ginger, garlic and sake, coated in potato starch (**片栗粉 · katakuriko**) or
  flour, and often **fried twice** for an extra-crisp crust. It comes with a lemon
  wedge. Ask before squeezing it over a shared plate: whether lemon belongs on
  karaage is a famous Japanese table debate.
- **串カツ · kushikatsu**: skewers of meat, seafood and vegetables, breaded and
  fried, an Osaka (Shinsekai) specialty. The sauce is shared, so the rule is
  **二度漬け禁止 · nidozuke kinshi**, "no double dipping". Dip once; to add more
  sauce, scoop it with a piece of the free raw cabbage.
- **コロッケ · korokke** (croquettes of mashed potato and minced meat) and
  **エビフライ · ebi furai** (breaded fried prawns) belong to *yōshoku* (section 7).

### Grilled 焼き物 · yakimono

- **焼き鳥 · yakitori**: chicken on bamboo skewers, grilled over hot, nearly
  smokeless **備長炭 · binchōtan** charcoal. You choose **タレ · tare** (a sweet
  soy glaze; old shops keep the same pot going for years, topping it up) or
  **塩 · shio** (salt). Parts to know:

  | Skewer | Part |
  |--------|------|
  | もも · momo | thigh |
  | ねぎま · negima | thigh alternating with leek |
  | つくね · tsukune | chicken meatball, often dipped in raw egg yolk |
  | かわ · kawa | skin, grilled crisp |
  | ささみ · sasami | tender breast fillet |
  | 手羽先 · tebasaki | wing |
  | せせり · seseri | neck meat |
  | 砂肝 · sunagimo (also ずり · zuri) | gizzard, crunchy |
  | レバー · rebā | liver |
  | ハツ · hatsu | heart |
  | ぼんじり · bonjiri | tail (very fatty) |
  | 軟骨 · nankotsu | cartilage, crunchy |

  Skewers called *yakitori* also include pork (e.g. **豚バラ · buta bara**,
  pork belly) and vegetables (**椎茸 · shiitake**, **ししとう · shishitō** peppers,
  **銀杏 · ginnan** ginkgo nuts). Some places serve **sasami** or liver barely
  cooked: rare chicken is a known food-poisoning risk (*Campylobacter*), so ask
  for it well done if you prefer: *Yoku yaite kudasai*.
- **焼肉 · yakiniku**: Korean-style barbecue that you grill yourself at the table.
  Cuts: **カルビ · karubi** (fatty short rib/belly), **ロース · rōsu** (leaner
  loin), **タン · tan** (beef tongue, eaten first, with salt and lemon),
  **ハラミ · harami** (skirt), **ホルモン · horumon** (offal). Use the tongs for raw
  meat and your chopsticks only for cooked.
- **和牛 · wagyū** means one of **four native Japanese cattle breeds** (mostly
  Japanese Black), not a cooking style. The grade **A5** combines a *yield grade*
  (A–C, A best) with a *meat-quality grade* (1–5, 5 best, judged mostly on
  marbling). **神戸ビーフ · Kōbe bīfu** is a protected brand: Tajima-line Japanese
  Black cattle born and raised in Hyōgo, meeting strict grading rules. Abroad, much
  "Kobe beef" is not genuine; in Japan, certified shops display the brand's mark.
- **鰻の蒲焼 · unagi no kabayaki**: freshwater eel, split, skewered and grilled
  over charcoal while basted in a sweet soy sauce, then sprinkled with sanshō.
  **Kantō style** splits the eel along the back and **steams it before grilling**,
  for a soft, melting texture. **Kansai style** splits it from the belly and grills
  it **without steaming**, so it stays crisp and firmer. Nagoya's
  **ひつまぶし · hitsumabushi** serves chopped grilled eel on rice to eat in three
  ways: plain, with condiments (spring onion, wasabi, nori), then with dashi poured
  over. Eel is traditionally eaten on **土用の丑の日 · doyō no ushi no hi**, a
  midsummer day, for stamina. The Japanese eel is listed as **endangered**, and
  prices reflect it.
- **照り焼き · teriyaki**: "glossy grill". Fish or chicken is grilled, then
  glazed with soy sauce, mirin, sake and sugar reduced until shiny. In Japan the
  classic is **鰤の照り焼き · buri no teriyaki** (yellowtail), not the sweet sauce
  sold abroad.
- **塩焼き · shioyaki**: whole or filleted fish, salted and grilled; the everyday
  way with **鯖 · saba** (mackerel) and, in autumn, **秋刀魚 · sanma** (Pacific saury).
- **炙り · aburi**: sushi or sashimi seared with a blowtorch. **鰹のたたき ·
  katsuo no tataki** (Kōchi) is skipjack tuna seared over a fierce straw fire,
  sliced thick and eaten rare with garlic, onion and ponzu.

### Griddle food 鉄板焼き · teppanyaki and *konamon*

**粉もん · konamon** ("flour things") is Osaka's word for its batter-based street food.

- **お好み焼き · okonomiyaki**: "grill what you like".
  - **Osaka style**: a batter of flour, dashi, egg and grated **長芋 · nagaimo**
    (a mountain yam that makes it fluffy) is **mixed together** with lots of
    shredded cabbage and the fillings (pork belly, squid, prawn), cooked as a thick
    pancake on a hotplate and flipped to cook both sides. It's finished with sweet-savoury
    **お好みソース · okonomi sōsu**, mayonnaise, aonori and dancing bonito flakes.
  - **Hiroshima style**: built in **layers**, never mixed: a thin crêpe of batter,
    then a tall pile of cabbage, bean sprouts and pork, flipped so it steams;
    a layer of fried noodles (yakisoba or udon) and a fried egg go underneath, and
    it's sauced on top. Ordered by noodle type: *soba-iri* or *udon-iri*.
- **もんじゃ焼き · monjayaki** (Tokyo, Tsukishima): a very runny batter poured into
  a ring of fried chopped ingredients on the griddle. It never sets firm; you
  scrape the crisping edges off the hotplate with a tiny spatula (**はがし ·
  hagashi**) and eat straight from it.
- **たこ焼き · takoyaki**: balls of thin, dashi-rich batter with a piece of boiled
  octopus, tenkasu, pickled ginger and spring onion, cooked in a cast-iron pan of
  hemispherical moulds and turned quickly with picks so they become round. Crisp
  outside, **molten inside**: bite carefully. Topped like okonomiyaki.
- **明石焼き · akashiyaki** (Akashi, Hyōgo): a softer, egg-rich cousin of
  takoyaki, dipped in warm dashi instead of sauce.
- **餃子 · gyōza**: dumplings of minced pork, cabbage, garlic chives and garlic.
  **焼き餃子 · yaki gyōza** are pan-fried on one side, then water is added and the
  pan covered so they steam, then uncovered so the bottoms fry crisp again. A
  thin starch slurry gives them a lacy "wing" (**羽根つき · hanetsuki**). Dip in a
  mix of soy sauce, vinegar and chilli oil (**ラー油 · rāyu**) that you make
  yourself. **水餃子 · sui gyōza** are boiled. Utsunomiya and Hamamatsu compete for
  the title of gyōza capital.

### Hot pots 鍋 · nabe and simmered dishes 煮物 · nimono

Hot pots are cooked at the table, shared, and usually end with rice
(**雑炊 · zōsui**, a soupy rice porridge) or udon added to the remaining broth:
the best part.

- **すき焼き · sukiyaki**: thin slices of fatty beef cooked in a shallow iron pan
  with tofu, spring onion, shiitake, chrysanthemum leaves (**春菊 · shungiku**) and
  shirataki, in a sweet soy sauce. Each bite is dipped in **beaten raw egg**, which
  cools and coats it (Japanese eggs are produced to be eaten raw; if you'd rather
  not, just skip the dip). **Kantō** style simmers everything in a pre-mixed sauce
  (**割下 · warishita**: soy, mirin, sugar, sake). **Kansai** style first sears the
  beef with sugar and soy directly in the pan, then adds the vegetables.
- **しゃぶしゃぶ · shabu-shabu**: paper-thin beef or pork, swished a few times
  through simmering kelp broth ("shabu-shabu" is the swishing sound) and dipped in
  **ponzu** or creamy **ごまだれ · gomadare** (sesame sauce). Skim the grey foam
  (**灰汁 · aku**) as it rises.
- **おでん · oden**: a winter dish of ingredients simmered for hours in a light
  dashi: **大根 · daikon** (thick rounds of radish), boiled **卵 · tamago** (egg),
  **こんにゃく · konnyaku** (a firm jelly from konjac root), **ちくわ · chikuwa**
  (tube of grilled fish paste), **はんぺん · hanpen** (fluffy fish cake),
  **がんもどき · ganmodoki** (fried tofu fritter) and **牛すじ · gyūsuji** (beef
  tendon), with hot mustard. Konbini sell it from a heated tray by the till in the
  cold months; **静岡おでん · Shizuoka oden** is dark, in a beef-stock broth, and
  sprinkled with fish powder.
- **ちゃんこ鍋 · chanko nabe**: the sumo wrestlers' high-protein hot pot (try it
  near the Ryōgoku sumo hall in Tokyo). **もつ鍋 · motsunabe** (Fukuoka): beef or
  pork offal with cabbage, garlic chives and garlic in a soy or miso broth.
- **肉じゃが · nikujaga**: home-style simmer of beef (or pork), potatoes, onion
  and carrot in dashi, soy, mirin and sugar; the archetypal "mother's cooking".

## 5. Soups, eggs, tofu and side dishes

- **味噌汁 · misoshiru · miso soup**: dashi with tofu, wakame seaweed, spring
  onion or clams. The miso is dissolved in at the end and **never boiled**:
  boiling dulls its aroma. **豚汁 · tonjiru** adds pork and root vegetables.
  Drink straight from the bowl and use chopsticks for the solids.
- **お吸い物 · osuimono**: clear soup of fine dashi, lightly salted, with a few
  carefully cut ingredients; part of formal meals.
- **茶碗蒸し · chawanmushi**: savoury egg custard steamed in a cup, with shrimp,
  chicken, ginkgo nuts and shiitake hidden inside. About **one part egg to three
  parts dashi**, steamed over gentle heat: too hot and it fills with bubbles
  (**す · su**) and turns rough.
- **卵焼き · tamagoyaki**: an omelette rolled up layer by layer in a small
  rectangular pan. **Kantō** versions are sweet; **Kansai**'s
  **だし巻き卵 · dashimaki tamago** is softer, juicier and savoury, made with plenty
  of dashi.
- **納豆 · nattō**: soybeans fermented with *Bacillus subtilis* (natto bacteria),
  sticky, stringy and pungent. Stir it vigorously (the strings multiply), add the
  sachets of mustard and sauce, and eat over rice, usually at breakfast. More
  popular in eastern Japan than in the west.
- **Tofu 豆腐 · tōfu**: **木綿 · momen** (firm, pressed in cloth-lined boxes) and
  **絹ごし · kinugoshi** (silken, set without pressing). **冷奴 · hiyayakko** is
  chilled tofu with ginger, spring onion and soy. **揚げ出し豆腐 · agedashi dōfu**
  is tofu dusted in starch, fried and served in warm dashi. **油揚げ · aburaage**
  is thin fried tofu (the pouch for inarizushi); **厚揚げ · atsuage** is thick.
  **湯葉 · yuba** is the skin lifted off heated soy milk (a Kyoto and Nikkō
  speciality). **湯豆腐 · yudōfu** is tofu warmed in kelp broth (Kyoto).
- **枝豆 · edamame**: young soybeans boiled in their pods and salted; squeeze the
  beans straight into your mouth and leave the pods.
- **お浸し · ohitashi** (blanched greens, usually spinach, soaked in seasoned dashi),
  **胡麻和え · goma-ae** (vegetables in sweet sesame dressing),
  **酢の物 · sunomono** (cucumber, seaweed or octopus in a light vinegar dressing),
  **きんぴらごぼう · kinpira gobō** (burdock root and carrot stir-fried with soy,
  sugar and chilli), **ひじき · hijiki** (black seaweed simmered with carrot and
  fried tofu).

## 6. Seafood and delicacies

- **刺身 · sashimi**: raw fish and seafood sliced to suit each fish (thicker for
  tuna, paper-thin for firm white fish), served with grated daikon, shiso leaves
  and wasabi. Put a little wasabi on the fish, then dip it lightly in soy sauce;
  don't stir the wasabi into the soy sauce at a good restaurant.
- **河豚 · fugu · pufferfish**: its organs contain a lethal nerve poison
  (tetrodotoxin). Only **licensed chefs** may prepare it, and restaurants are
  inspected. Eaten as translucent sashimi (**てっさ · tessa**) arranged like a
  chrysanthemum, in hot pot (**てっちり · tetchiri**), and fried. Winter is the
  season; Shimonoseki is the centre. Never eat fugu from an unlicensed source.
- **蟹 · kani · crab**: Hokkaidō and the Sea of Japan coast in winter:
  **ずわい蟹 · zuwaigani** (snow crab; the males are sold as **松葉蟹 · matsubagani**
  in San'in and **越前蟹 · echizengani** in Fukui), **毛蟹 · kegani** (hairy crab),
  **タラバ蟹 · tarabagani** (red king crab).
- **牡蠣 · kaki · oysters**: Hiroshima is the main producer; eaten fried
  (**カキフライ · kaki furai**), grilled, or raw (if labelled **生食用 ·
  namashokuyō**, "for eating raw").
- **明太子 · mentaiko**: pollock roe cured with chilli, a Fukuoka speciality, on
  rice, in rice balls and in pasta.
- More adventurous: **白子 · shirako** (cod milt), **あん肝 · ankimo** (monkfish
  liver, "the foie gras of the sea"), **ホタルイカ · hotaru ika** (firefly squid,
  spring), **馬刺し · basashi** (raw horse meat, Kumamoto).
- **鶏刺し · torisashi** (raw or barely seared chicken, Kagoshima and Miyazaki):
  traditional, but Japan's health ministry warns that it causes many cases of
  *Campylobacter* food poisoning. Your call.

## 7. Meal formats and where to eat them

| Format | What it is |
|--------|------------|
| **定食 · teishoku** | A set meal on a tray: one main (grilled fish, fried pork, ginger pork **生姜焼き · shōgayaki**) with rice, miso soup and pickles. Cheap and balanced; rice refills are often free (**おかわり自由 · okawari jiyū**). |
| **弁当 · bentō** | A boxed meal. **駅弁 · ekiben** are station bentō with regional specialities, made to eat on the train (eating on long-distance trains is fine). **幕の内弁当 · makunouchi bentō** has rice and a little of everything. |
| **居酒屋 · izakaya** | A pub that serves many small shared dishes. A small **お通し · otōshi** starter arrives unasked and is charged as a cover (see Lesson 8). Order drinks first, then dishes a few at a time. **飲み放題 · nomihōdai** / **食べ放題 · tabehōdai** mean all-you-can-drink / all-you-can-eat, always with a time limit (often 90–120 minutes) and a **L.O.** (last order) before it. |
| **懐石 / 会席 · kaiseki** | Japan's multi-course haute cuisine. **懐石** (written with "breast-stone") was originally the simple meal served at the tea ceremony; **会席** ("gathering seats") is the banquet version served at ryokan and restaurants. Both are pronounced *kaiseki*. A typical sequence: an appetiser (**先付 · sakizuke**), a seasonal platter (**八寸 · hassun**), sashimi (**向付 · mukōzuke**), a simmered dish (**煮物 · nimono** or **炊き合わせ · takiawase**), a grilled dish (**焼物 · yakimono**), then rice with miso soup and pickles, and fruit or a sweet. Dishes are arranged to show the season. |
| **精進料理 · shōjin ryōri** | Buddhist temple cuisine: no meat or fish, traditionally no garlic or onions either. Built on tofu, sesame, vegetables, mountain plants and kelp dashi. The best option for vegetarians and vegans; served at temples in Kyoto, Kōyasan and elsewhere. |
| **立ち食い · tachigui** | Stand-up counters, especially soba at stations: order, eat in five minutes, go. |
| **屋台 · yatai** | Street food stalls with a few seats, famous in Fukuoka (ramen, oden, yakitori by the river at night). |
| **デパ地下 · depachika** | Department store basement food halls: bentō, sushi, sweets and gift boxes. Prepared food is often discounted in the last hour before closing (look for 割引 · *waribiki* stickers). |
| **喫茶店 · kissaten** | Old-style coffee shops. Order the **モーニング · mōningu** (morning set) before about 11:00: coffee with thick toast and a boiled egg, at little more than the price of the coffee. Nagoya is famous for it. |
| **ファミレス · famiresu** | Family restaurants (Gusto, Saizeriya, Royal Host): cheap, picture menus, a drink bar. |

**洋食 · yōshoku, Western food Japanese-style.** Dishes adopted from Europe
during the Meiji era and made Japanese: **ハンバーグ · hanbāgu** (a juicy
minced-meat steak with demi-glace sauce), **オムライス · omuraisu**,
**ナポリタン · naporitan** (spaghetti fried with ketchup, onion, green pepper and
sausage), **コロッケ · korokke**, **エビフライ · ebi furai**,
**ドリア · doria** (rice gratin) and **カレーライス**. They're comfort food, not
imitations, and worth trying.

## 8. Sweets and drinks

### Sweets 和菓子 · wagashi

Traditional sweets are built on **餡 · an** (also **あんこ · anko**), a paste of
**小豆 · azuki** beans simmered with sugar: **粒餡 · tsubuan** is chunky, with
the skins; **こし餡 · koshian** is sieved smooth.

| Sweet | What it is |
|-------|------------|
| **大福 · daifuku** | Soft mochi filled with anko. **苺大福 · ichigo daifuku** hides a whole strawberry. |
| **団子 · dango** | Skewered balls of rice-flour dumpling. **みたらし · mitarashi**: grilled, in a sweet soy glaze. **花見団子 · hanami dango**: pink, white and green, for cherry-blossom season. |
| **どら焼き · dorayaki** | Two small honey pancakes sandwiching anko. |
| **たい焼き · taiyaki** | A fish-shaped waffle filled with anko (or custard), baked in an iron mould. |
| **わらび餅 · warabimochi** | Wobbly jelly made from bracken starch, dusted with roasted soybean flour (**きな粉 · kinako**). |
| **羊羹 · yōkan** | A firm block of anko set with agar. |
| **最中 · monaka** | Anko sandwiched between two crisp wafers of mochi. |
| **練り切り · nerikiri** | Delicate seasonal shapes (flowers, leaves) of sweetened white bean paste, served with matcha at the tea ceremony. |
| **かき氷 · kakigōri** | Shaved ice with syrup, in summer; **宇治金時 · uji kintoki** is matcha syrup with sweet beans. |
| **煎餅 · senbei** | Crisp rice crackers, usually savoury with soy sauce. |

Also look out for **カステラ · kasutera** (a moist sponge cake from Nagasaki,
originally Portuguese), and the many **matcha** and seasonal flavours in konbini
and cafés.

### Tea お茶 · ocha

Japanese tea is green tea, steamed right after picking (which keeps it green and
grassy) rather than pan-fired as in China.

| Tea | What it is | Brewing |
|-----|------------|---------|
| **煎茶 · sencha** | The everyday green tea | Water at about 70–80 °C, about a minute; boiling water makes it bitter. |
| **玉露 · gyokuro** | Shaded for weeks before picking: sweet, intensely savoury | Cool water, about 50–60 °C, small amounts. |
| **抹茶 · matcha** | Shaded leaves (*tencha*), stone-ground to powder | Not steeped: **whisked** with hot water and a bamboo whisk (**茶筅 · chasen**) into a frothy bowl. The tea of the tea ceremony. |
| **ほうじ茶 · hōjicha** | Roasted green tea: brown, toasty, low in caffeine | Boiling water is fine. |
| **玄米茶 · genmaicha** | Green tea with toasted rice grains | Nutty; hot water. |
| **麦茶 · mugicha** | Roasted barley "tea", no caffeine | Drunk ice-cold in summer, often free at restaurants. |

Free tea or water arrives automatically in most restaurants. In vending machines,
**つめたい** = cold and **あたたかい** = hot (Lesson 2).

### Sake 日本酒 · nihonshu

In Japanese, *sake* (酒) means any alcohol; the drink you know as sake is
**日本酒 · nihonshu**. It's brewed from rice, water, kōji and yeast. Kōji turns
the rice starch into sugar **at the same time** as the yeast turns that sugar into
alcohol (*multiple parallel fermentation*), which is how sake reaches about 15–16%
alcohol without distillation.

The grade depends on how much of each rice grain has been **polished away** (the
outer layers carry fats and proteins that give rougher flavours). The remaining
percentage is the **精米歩合 · seimai buai** printed on the label:

| Label | Rice remaining | Brewer's alcohol added? |
|-------|----------------|-------------------------|
| **純米 · junmai** | no legal minimum | no |
| **本醸造 · honjōzō** | ≤ 70% | a small amount |
| **吟醸 · ginjō** / **純米吟醸 · junmai ginjō** | ≤ 60% | small amount / none |
| **大吟醸 · daiginjō** / **純米大吟醸 · junmai daiginjō** | ≤ 50% | small amount / none |

Ginjō and daiginjō are fermented cool and slowly: fruity and fragrant, best
chilled. Other words on labels and menus: **生 · nama** (unpasteurised, fresh,
keep it cold), **にごり · nigori** (cloudy, coarsely filtered), **原酒 · genshu**
(undiluted, stronger), **辛口 · karakuchi** (dry), **甘口 · amakuchi** (sweet).
Served **冷酒 · reishu** (chilled), **冷や · hiya** (traditionally room
temperature), **ぬる燗 · nurukan** (warm, about 40 °C) or **熱燗 · atsukan** (hot,
about 50 °C), from a flask (**徳利 · tokkuri**) into small cups. Some bars pour
into a glass standing in a wooden box (**升 · masu**) until it overflows, as a
sign of generosity.

### Other drinks

- **焼酎 · shōchū**: a **distilled** spirit (usually 25%), made from sweet potato
  (**芋 · imo**, Kagoshima), barley (**麦 · mugi**), rice (**米 · kome**) and
  more. Drunk on the rocks, with water (**水割り · mizuwari**), with hot water
  (**お湯割り · oyuwari**), or in mixed drinks.
- **泡盛 · awamori**: Okinawa's spirit, distilled from long-grain rice with black
  kōji; aged versions are called **古酒 · kūsu**.
- **チューハイ / サワー · chūhai / sawā**: shōchū (or another neutral spirit) with
  soda and fruit, e.g. **レモンサワー · remon sawā**. Cans in konbini can be 9%:
  stronger than beer.
- **ハイボール · haibōru**: whisky and soda, the standard izakaya order.
- **梅酒 · umeshu**: ume fruit steeped in spirit with sugar; sweet and fruity.
- **生ビール · nama bīru**: draught beer, the default first order ("*Toriaezu
  nama!*", "a draught to start with"). The big brewers are Asahi, Kirin,
  Sapporo and Suntory.

| 季節限定 | kisetsu gentei | seasonal, limited time |
| 自家製 | jikasei | house-made |
| 国産 | kokusan | produced in Japan |
| 地元 / 地鶏 | jimoto / jidori | local / local free-range chicken breed |
| 時価 | jika | **market price**: ask the price before ordering |
| 並 · 大盛 · 特盛 | nami · ōmori · tokumori | regular · large · extra-large portion |
| 大盛無料 | ōmori muryō | large portion at no extra charge |
| おかわり自由 | okawari jiyū | free refills (usually rice, cabbage or tea) |
| 替え玉 | kaedama | extra noodles (ramen) |
| セット | setto | set (with rice, soup, a drink…) |
| 単品 | tanpin | à la carte, the dish alone |
| 辛口 · 甘口 | karakuchi · amakuchi | spicy or dry · mild or sweet |
| 激辛 | gekikara | extremely spicy |
| 食べ放題 · 飲み放題 | tabehōdai · nomihōdai | all you can eat · all you can drink |
| ラストオーダー / L.O. | rasuto ōdā | last order |

Plastic food models in the window (**食品サンプル · shokuhin sanpuru**) show
exactly what you'll get, at the price shown. If there's no English menu, you can
take the waiter outside and point: *Kore o kudasai* (this one, please).

**Useful lines**

- *Osusume wa nan desu ka?* · What do you recommend?
- *Kore wa nan desu ka?* · What is this?
- *Kore wa nani ga haitte imasu ka?* · What's in this?
- *Onaji mono o kudasai.* · The same, please (pointing at another table's dish).
- *Ōmori de onegaishimasu.* · A large portion, please.
- *Katame de onegaishimasu.* · Firm noodles, please.
- *Kara-sa wa erabemasu ka?* · Can I choose the spiciness?
- *Mō hitotsu onegaishimasu.* · One more, please.
- *Oishii desu!* · It's delicious! (Staff love hearing it.)
- *Gochisōsama deshita.* · Thank you for the meal (as you leave).

**Dietary needs** (see also Lesson 5):

- **Vegetarians and vegans**: dashi made with bonito is in miso soup, noodle
  broths, simmered dishes and many sauces, so "vegetable" dishes are often not
  vegetarian. Ask *Katsuobushi no dashi wa haitte imasu ka?* (Is there bonito
  stock in it?). Look for **shōjin ryōri** and for vegan ramen shops in big cities.
- **Gluten**: soy sauce, most miso blends, tempura, ramen, udon, curry roux and
  many sauces contain wheat. Cross-contamination is common. Carry a card in
  Japanese.
- **Allergens**: packaged food in Japan must list eight major allergens
  (**特定原材料 · tokutei genzairyō**): **えび** prawn/shrimp, **かに** crab,
  **くるみ** walnut, **小麦** wheat, **そば** buckwheat, **卵** egg, **乳** milk,
  **落花生** peanut. Restaurants aren't required to label them, so always ask.
- **Pork and alcohol**: mirin and cooking sake are in many sauces, and lard or pork
  stock in many broths. A small but growing number of restaurants are **halal**
  or "Muslim-friendly" certified.

## 11. Practice

1. You order *zaru soba*. What arrives, and what do you do with the pot of hot
   liquid at the end?
2. What's the difference between sushi and sashimi?
3. Which ramen broth is milky white, and how is it made?
4. Your okonomiyaki has noodles in it and was built in layers. Which city's
   style is it?
5. A menu item's price says **時価**. What should you do?
6. You're vegetarian. Why is *kake udon* probably not suitable, and which kind of
   cuisine is?
7. Which Japanese tea is whisked rather than steeped?
8. What does **純米大吟醸** on a sake label tell you?

<details>
<summary><strong>Answers</strong></summary>

1. Chilled buckwheat noodles on a bamboo tray with cold dipping sauce. The pot is
   **sobayu**, the noodle cooking water: pour it into your leftover sauce and drink it.
2. *Sushi* is the vinegared rice (with toppings); *sashimi* is sliced raw fish without rice.
3. **Tonkotsu** (pork-bone paitan): the bones are boiled hard for many hours until fat
   and collagen emulsify into the broth.
4. **Hiroshima**.
5. It's **market price**: ask how much it is before ordering (*Ikura desu ka?*).
6. Its broth is usually made with **bonito** dashi. **Shōjin ryōri**, Buddhist
   temple cuisine, has no meat or fish.
7. **Matcha**.
8. No added alcohol (*junmai*), and at most **50%** of each rice grain remains after
   polishing (*daiginjō*): a premium, fragrant sake, best served chilled.
