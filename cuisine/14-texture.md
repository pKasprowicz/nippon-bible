# 14. The Language of Texture

Every chapter of this handbook has ended up talking about texture: elastic pre-rigor fish, a dry gluten crust, sticky leached amylopectin, swelling udon starch, a calcium-bridged tofu gel, intact bean cells in a sugar–agar matrix. Japanese has an unusually large vocabulary for describing these sensations, and much of it is **onomatopoeic**: words that imitate sounds or that symbolise a sensation through sound. This final chapter presents what research has found about that vocabulary, so that a reader can understand how Japanese cooks and eaters **name** the textures this handbook has explained.

## 14.1 Why Japanese has so many texture words

Hanada's study of food-texture onomatopoeia reviews the counts in several languages (Hanada 2020):

| Study | Language | Texture terms |
|---|---|---|
| Szczesniak and Kleyn (1963) | English | 77 |
| Rohm (1990) | German | 105 |
| Lawless et al. (1997) | Finnish | 71 |
| Nishinari et al. (2008) | French / Chinese | 227 / 144 |
| Yoshikawa et al. (1970) | Japanese | 406 |
| Hayakawa et al. (2005) | Japanese | 445, about **70%** onomatopoeic |

In his discussion, Hanada also cites Hayakawa and colleagues' finding that **312 of 446** representative Japanese texture terms are onomatopoeic (Hanada 2020). Since the other languages do not have many onomatopoeic texture words, the use of onomatopoeia may be one reason Japanese has so many texture terms (Hanada 2020, citing Bourne 2002).

### Two kinds of onomatopoeia

Hanada explains that Japanese onomatopoeic words are generally divided into two groups (Hanada 2020):

- ***Giongo***, which **imitate real sounds**;
- ***Gitaigo***, which **symbolically depict** non-auditory impressions, states, movements or events through sound.

He adds several points important for a non-Japanese reader (Hanada 2020):

- A dictionary of onomatopoeic words edited by Yamaguchi (2015) lists about **2,000** words, and unconventional ones are often coined.
- Derivatives are formed by **lengthening a vowel**, adding a **doubled consonant**, or **reduplicating** the word. The resulting families have similar meanings with subtly different connotations.
- In English, sound-imitating expressions are often considered **childish**. In Japanese, onomatopoeic words are **not** regarded as immature, and they appear in newspaper articles and novels, although they can be colloquial.
- Many texture words also describe **touch**. *Surusuru*, for example, expresses both a smooth food texture and the feel of a smooth surface.
- Some words, such as *sakusaku* and *shariQ*, express not only the sound of chewing but also the **feeling** of it.

## 14.2 What Japanese eaters actually say

Hanada showed **50 Japanese university students** photographs of **56 foods**, Japanese dishes and raw foods, and asked them to write onomatopoeic words fitting each food's texture. The participants produced **771** different words. The most frequent were (Hanada 2020):

| Word | Times reported | Word | Times reported |
|---|---|---|---|
| *sakusaku* | 290 | *torotoro* | 101 |
| *tsurutsuru* | 242 | *poripori* | 98 |
| *shakishaki* | 224 | *juwajuwa* | 89 |
| *paripari* | 205 | *shiQtori* | 88 |
| *mochimochi* | 196 | *mosamosa* | 85 |
| *fuwafuwa* | 157 | *purupuru* | 82 |
| *zakuzaku* | 151 | *nebaneba* | 51 |

*In Hanada's transcription, "Q" marks a doubled consonant, so* shiQtori *is pronounced* shittori*.*

## 14.3 Fifteen dimensions of texture

Hanada analysed which words went with which foods and extracted **15 dimensions**, which together explained about 60% of the variation. The table below gives each dimension with some of the foods and words that defined it, and the meanings Hanada gives (Hanada 2020, Table 3).

| # | Texture dimension | Example foods | Example words (meaning) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Smoothness and sound of **slurping noodles** | ramen, udon | *zuruzuru*, *churuchuru* (slippery feel and sound in slurping), *surusuru* (smooth and slippery) |
| 2 | **Fluffiness, crumbliness, dryness** of breads | white bread, bean-jam bun, doughnut | *fuwafuwa*, *fukafuka* (soft and fluffy), *pasapasa*, *bosoboso* (dry and crumbly) |
| 3 | **Adhesiveness** | fermented soybeans (*nattō*) | *nebaneba* (sticky, adhesive, viscous), *nurunuru* (slimy and slippery), *betobeto* (sticky) |
| 4 | **Popping and juiciness** of round fruit and vegetables | cherry tomato, grapes | *puchiQ*, *puchi* (sound and feel of popping) |
| 5 | **Soft elasticity and smoothness of gels** | jelly, **tofu**, ***chawanmushi*** (savoury egg custard) | *purupuru* (soft, elastic, wobbly), *tsuruN*, *tsururi* (smooth and slippery) |
| 6 | **Wateriness, creaminess, coldness** | ice cream | *hiNyari* (cold), *dorodoro* (thick and viscous) |
| 7 | **Bendability of sticky foods** | gummy sweets, caramels, ***yōkan*** | *neQtori* (sticky and viscous), *gunyagunya*, *guniguni* (flexible and limp) |
| 8 | **Crispness and crunchiness of snacks** (and, at the other end, hot crumbly foods) | rice crackers, crisps; *opposite end:* sweet potato, roast salmon | *paripori*, *baribari*, *zakuzaku*; *opposite end:* *hokuhoku* (hot), *hokohoko* (soft, hot, crumbly) |
| 9 | **Crispness of fruit** | watermelon, apple, melon | *shakiQ*, *sharishari* (crisp), *zakuQ* (crunchy) |
| 10 | **Elasticity** | ***daifuku*** (mochi with bean paste), pizza, **cooked rice** | *nobinobi* (stretchy), *mochimochi* (sticky, elastic, chewy) |
| 11 | Texture specific to **lemon** | lemon | — |
| 12 | **Crunchiness of vegetables** | lettuce, celery, dried daikon strips | *shakishaki* (crisp feel and sound in biting), *jakijaki*, *koriQ* (crunchy) |
| 13 | **Snapping of rod-shaped foods** | pretzel, cucumber, asparagus | *pokiQ*, *pakiQ*, *pakipaki* (sound and feel of snapping) |
| 14 | **Sizzle**: heat and oiliness | *okonomiyaki*, sausage, *kakuni* (braised pork) | *jū-jū* (sizzling), *AtsuAtsu* (hot), *koQteri* (thick and oily), *hafuhafu* (breathing in to cool hot food) |
| 15 | Texture of **pickled plum** (*umeboshi*) | pickled plum | *shiwashiwa* (wrinkly), *guchagucha* (pulpy) |

### What the dimensions show

Hanada draws several conclusions (Hanada 2020):

- The dimensions do **not** match single physical properties. They represent **combinations** of properties. Dimension 5 relates to both softness and elasticity, and dimension 14 to both oiliness and warmth.
- Japanese texture onomatopoeia seems **better suited** to **mechanical** properties, **oil and fat**, and **water content** than to geometrical properties such as particle size and shape.
- **Hardness** did not appear as a distinct dimension, probably because especially hard foods were not included. Hanada notes that Hayakawa and colleagues (2013) also found no category specifically for hardness and firmness.

### Sound symbolism

Hanada observes that words in the same dimension often **share sounds** (Hanada 2020):

- **Stickiness** words (dimension 3) begin with ***n*** or ***b***: *nebaneba*, *betobeto*.
- **Gel** words (dimension 5) begin with ***p***, ***t*** or ***s***. Citing Hamano (1986), he notes that *p* often symbolises tearing a surface under tension, *t* weak tension, and *s* sliding on a smooth surface.
- **Bendable sticky** foods (dimension 7) have words beginning with ***g***: *gunyagunya*, *guniguni*.
- **Fruit crispness** (dimension 9) is carried by ***sh*** and ***z***: *shakiQ*, *zakuQ*.
- **Snapping** (dimension 13) by ***p***: *pokiQ*, *pakiQ*.

## 14.4 The vocabulary meets the handbook

The study connects directly to foods and structures covered in earlier chapters. Only the links supported by Hanada's data are listed here:

| Hanada's dimension | Food in the study | Structure explained in this handbook |
|---|---|---|
| 1, slurping noodles | udon | Ch. 10: starch swelling and the moisture gradient of a cooked noodle |
| 5, soft elastic gel | tofu, chawanmushi | Ch. 11: the protein gel formed by coagulants |
| 7, bendable sticky foods | yōkan | Ch. 12: bean cells in a sugar–agar gel |
| 10, elasticity, *mochimochi* | cooked rice, daifuku | Ch. 9: leached amylopectin; Ch. 13: waxy rice and mochi |
| 3, adhesiveness, *nebaneba* | nattō | (not covered in detail) |
| 8, crisp snacks | rice crackers | Ch. 7: a dry crust is crisp |

Pokharel and colleagues, in Chapter 10, describe ideal udon as soft, elastic and smooth, using exactly this vocabulary: ***mochi-mochi*** (Pokharel et al. 2023). The word that Hanada's participants used for cooked rice and daifuku is the same one food scientists use to define the target texture of a wheat noodle.
