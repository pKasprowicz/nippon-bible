# 8. Marinating with Kōji: Enzymes as a Cooking Tool

Chapters 3 to 5 showed kōji enzymes working on grain and beans inside a brewery. This chapter brings them into the kitchen. A marinade of ***shio-kōji*** (salted kōji) or other kōji seasonings is not just a flavouring. It carries **active enzymes** that keep working on the meat or fish they cover. The research on this is young, and the chapter reports exactly what has and has not been measured.

## 8.1 What shio-kōji is

Yamashita describes shio-kōji as kōji mixed with salt and water and fermented at room temperature for **7–10 days**. It has grown in popularity over the past decade. It contains fewer sugars than other seasonings, but its glucose, amino acids and salt make it an effective seasoning. Its most notable feature is that it is **rich in active kōji enzymes**, so meat soaked in it becomes **tender** and gains **umami** (Yamashita 2021).

Chien and colleagues give a technical description: shio-kōji comes from a **two-stage fermentation**. A grain such as rice is first inoculated with *Aspergillus oryzae* for **solid-state** fermentation (the kōji), and then blended with salt water for **liquid-state** fermentation. *A. oryzae* secretes highly active hydrolytic enzymes, **amylases and proteases**, which break carbohydrates, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids into small molecules such as glucose, amino acids, esters and nucleotides. According to the same source, this both adds flavour and **hydrolyses and softens muscle fibres**. Shio-kōji spread from Japan to Taiwan, where it is commonly used to marinate fish, and is reported to reduce fishy odour and increase umami and sweetness (Chien et al. 2026).

## 8.2 The evidence on meat

Chien and colleagues summarise earlier studies of kōji seasonings on meat (Chien et al. 2026):

- **Venison** treated with miso or shio-kōji had **lower cooking loss** and **lower puncture stress**, with a significant improvement in tenderness (Hiemori-Kondo et al. 2022).
- Soy sauce containing rice kōji tenderised **chicken breast** (Kim et al. 2014).
- In **smoked duck**, proteases from shio-kōji **hydrolysed the myofibrils** and softened the meat. Water-holding capacity also improved, which the authors attributed to shio-kōji promoting a protein network (Shen et al. 2019).
- **Pork** marinated in shio-kōji had **two to three times** the free amino acids and glutamic acid of unmarinated controls. Cooking loss was suppressed and juiciness preserved (Abe et al. 2018).

The pork result links directly to Chapter 1. Kōji proteases release **free glutamate** from protein inside the meat itself, so the marinade adds umami not only by coating the surface but by changing the meat's own chemistry.

### A controlled experiment on chicken breast

Jeong and colleagues cured chicken breast with four treatments, each tumbled under vacuum at 0 °C for 60 minutes, then held at **4 °C for 4 hours**, then cooked by superheated steam followed by a convection oven to a core temperature of at least 75 °C (Jeong et al. 2023):

- **GC:** a commercial curing agent, at 4% w/w;
- **FR:** *A. oryzae* grown on **rice**, at 5% w/w;
- **FS:** *A. oryzae* grown on **soybean**, at 5% w/w;
- **BS:** *Bacillus subtilis* grown on soybean, at 5% w/w (the authors group this with the kōji treatments, although the organism is a bacterium, not the kōji mould);
- **C:** uncured control.

Results (Jeong et al. 2023):

| Measure | Control | GC | FR (rice kōji) | FS (soybean kōji) | BS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hardness (N) | 66.3 | 61.7 | 56.8 | **52.8** | 57.7 |
| Expressible moisture (%) | 16.9 | 20.0 | 23.9 | **24.3** | 21.4 |
| Sensory umami (score) | 3.6 | 4.7 | 5.8 | 5.4 | **6.3** |
| Sensory tenderness (score) | 3.2 | 4.0 | 5.9 | 4.9 | **6.3** |

- All the fermented-grain treatments were significantly **less hard** than both the control and the commercial curing agent. Soybean kōji was the softest.
- The authors relate this to **protease activity**: in a prior test, *A. oryzae* on soybean had higher protease activity (311.2 units/g), and they cite earlier work showing soybean kōji has higher protease activity than rice kōji because of its higher protein content.
- **Expressible moisture**, the free water released under pressure and a measure related to juiciness, was significantly higher in the kōji treatments. The authors attribute this to changes in the myofibrillar protein matrix caused by the enzymes. They also note that higher expressible moisture can mean more drip loss.
- **Water content and cooking loss** did not differ significantly between any of the treatments.
- Tasters found **no difference in appearance or smell**, but rated the kōji-treated samples higher for umami, juiciness and tenderness.

> **Technique consequence.** In this study, a marinade of only 5% kōji by weight, held cold for just a few hours, measurably softened chicken breast and raised its perceived umami and juiciness. The softening is attributed to **proteases**, and the soybean-grown kōji, with more protease, softened most. A cook choosing a kōji seasoning to tenderise is choosing a **protease dose**.

## 8.3 The evidence on fish: squid

Chien and colleagues studied **Argentine squid** mantle (Chien et al. 2026). Their shio-kōji was rice kōji mixed with **12% salt water at 1:5 (w/w)** and incubated at **37 °C for 3 days**. The finished marinade had about **9.5% salt**, **pH 4.8** and a protease activity of about **200 U/mL**. It was spread on the squid at **3 parts squid to 1 part marinade** and vacuum-packed at **4 °C for 24 hours**.

Immediate effects of shio-kōji alone (Chien et al. 2026):

| Texture measure | Untreated | Shio-kōji |
|---|---|---|
| Hardness (N) | 28.6 | **15.1** |
| Chewiness (mJ) | 43.7 | **26.9** |
| Springiness (mm) | 2.76 | 2.46 |
| Resilience | 0.69 | **0.46** |

Shio-kōji **halved hardness** and markedly reduced chewiness and resilience within 24 hours. It also turned the squid from **translucent white to yellowish**, which the authors attribute to the milky yellow colour of the marinade itself (Chien et al. 2026).

The study's main subject was combining shio-kōji with **high-pressure processing** (600 MPa for 5 minutes), an industrial preservation technique. Pressure alone made the squid harder and whiter, giving it a partly cooked look. Shio-kōji partly offset these effects, and the combination inhibited microbial growth and delayed spoilage indicators over **30 days at 4 °C** (Chien et al. 2026). The authors also warn that improved texture and flavour from shio-kōji **do not in themselves indicate better preservation or chemical safety**, which must be measured separately (Chien et al. 2026).

> **Technique consequence.** On a delicate seafood such as squid, a day in shio-kōji halves the hardness. The same enzymes that tenderise tough chicken breast can over-soften a tender food. Strength of marinade, temperature and time are the cook's controls. The squid study ran at 4 °C for 24 hours; the chicken study at 4 °C for 4 hours.

## 8.4 Why kōji marinades work: putting it together

Drawing on Chapters 1, 3 and 4:

1. **Proteases cut muscle proteins.** This softens the texture (Jeong et al. 2023; Chien et al. 2026) and releases **free amino acids, including glutamate**, which add umami (Abe et al. 2018, cited in Chien et al. 2026).
2. **Amylases make sugars** from the grain in the kōji, contributing sweetness (Chien et al. 2026; Yamashita 2021).
3. **Salt** seasons, and in Jeong and colleagues' study the kōji-cured breasts **swelled** more during curing. The authors link such swelling to greater electrostatic repulsion between muscle filaments and to the breakdown of myosin filaments (Jeong et al. 2023).
4. **The enzymes stay active** in a shio-kōji that has not been heated (Yamashita 2021). This makes it different from a sauce: it is a reagent, and its effect depends on time and temperature.
