# Japanese Cuisine: A Technique Handbook

This handbook explains **how Japanese food is made to taste, feel and look the way it does**. It is not a recipe book. It follows the **processes** behind the ingredients and dishes: the moulds, enzymes, salts, starches, gels and temperatures. It asks, at each step, what the cook or maker is controlling and why it works.

It is written for a reader who wants to understand, and who is willing to read closely. Chapters are long, numbers are given where the sources give them, and disagreements between sources are shown rather than smoothed over.

## How this handbook treats facts

- **Every factual statement comes from a named source** in the chapter's source list. Citations appear in brackets, such as (Kurihara 2015).
- **Sources are peer-reviewed research** (from J-STAGE, PubMed Central and food-science journals), **official bodies** (Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, the European Food Safety Authority) and **producers' educational and technical bodies** (the Umami Information Center, the Institute for Fermentation, Osaka). Encyclopaedias, blogs and recipe sites are not used as sources.
- **Where the sources disagree**, both versions are given (see the katsuobushi chapter).
- **Where the sources are silent**, the handbook says so instead of filling the gap with common kitchen lore. Several chapters end with a short "what the sources do not cover" note.
- Some studies were done outside Japan, on ingredients or model foods rather than on the Japanese dish itself. When that is the case, the text says so.
- Some older Japanese papers are available only as scanned Japanese text. For those, the handbook cites only the English abstract and figure captions, and says so in the source list.

## Diagrams and further-reading links

- **Molecule diagrams.** Seven chapters include structural diagrams of the molecules they discuss, such as the three umami substances, the breakdown of ATP in fish muscle and the two links in starch. Each structure is drawn from its record in **PubChem**, the open chemistry database of the US National Library of Medicine, and the chemistry it illustrates is cited in the caption. A highlighted group marks the part of the molecule that the text is about. The [sources page](16-sources.md) lists every diagram with its PubChem records.
- **Links marked ↗** (for example [umami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umami)) lead to **Wikipedia** or PubChem, for background on a term, a person, an organism or a place. Each term is linked at its first appearance in a chapter. These links are **further reading only**. Nothing in the handbook rests on them; every claim still comes from the sources named in the text. They need an internet connection, even when the rest of the handbook is saved for offline use.
