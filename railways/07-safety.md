# 7. Safety and Punctuality

Japanese trains are famous for running on time. This chapter looks at what the official figures actually say, and at the accidents and disasters that shaped today's safety rules.

## 7.1 What "on time" means in numbers

The most widely quoted figure comes from JR Central. In fiscal 2024 the **average delay** on the Tōkaidō Shinkansen was **1.4 minutes per train**. That figure **includes** delays from natural disasters such as storms and earthquakes (JR Central 2025).

It is an average over all trains, so a single typhoon that stops trains for hours raises it. The sources used here do not give a comparable national figure for all Japanese railways.

## 7.2 Safety systems

The ministry's history records how protection was built up step by step (MLIT 2012):

- **1956:** an ordinance introduced **licences for train drivers**, with examinations and approved training schools.
- **1961:** the **Level Crossing Improvement Act**. Crossing accidents had peaked in fiscal 1960 and then fell as crossings were rebuilt, raised or fitted with equipment.
- **Fiscal 1965:** **automatic train stop** (ATS) was installed on all JNR lines.
- On the Shinkansen, the driver's speedometer doubled as the display of the **automatic train control** (ATC) system (picture in Chapter 3).

Since the JNR reform of 1987, the number of railway accidents on JR lines has fallen by 60% (MLIT 2017).

## 7.3 Accidents that changed the rules

**Shigaraki, May 1991.** On the Shigaraki Kōgen Railway, a train left Shigaraki station against a red signal (the signal had failed) without following the required procedure. It collided head-on with a JR West train running through onto the line: **42 people died and 614 were injured**. Because the cause was a failure to follow the most basic safety rules, the Ministry of Transport surveyed rule compliance and signalling systems nationwide and drew up new safety measures (MLIT 2012).

**Fukuchiyama line, April 2005.** At about 9:18 on 25 April 2005, a JR West rapid train between Tsukaguchi and Amagasaki entered a right-hand curve of 304 m radius, with a speed limit of 70 km/h, at **116 km/h**. The first five cars derailed, and the first two struck an apartment building. **106 passengers and the driver died; 562 passengers and one passer-by were injured** (JR West n.d.-a).

**JR Hokkaidō, 2013.** After a freight train derailed at Ōnuma station in September 2013, falsified inspection data were discovered. The ministry carried out three special safety audits and in January 2014 ordered the company to eradicate falsification and rebuild its safety management (MLIT 2017).

## 7.4 Earthquakes and attacks

**The Great Hanshin-Awaji Earthquake, January 1995.** Thirty-two railway bridges fell, concrete viaduct columns failed, and an underground station collapsed; about 640 km of line was cut. JR lines, including the Sanyō Shinkansen, were fully reopened by April 1995. The ministry then ordered urgent seismic strengthening of viaduct columns and tunnel pillars (MLIT 2012).

**The sarin attack, March 1995** (Chapter 6) led the ministry to ask operators for security cameras, patrols and evacuation manuals (MLIT 2012).

**Disasters now hit freight hardest.** The ministry lists one long disruption after another: in July 2018, for example, heavy rain cut the Sanyō line and others for **100 days**, cancelling 4,421 freight trains (MLIT 2025). Chapter 11 explains why.
